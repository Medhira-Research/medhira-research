# My First-Year Lessons: Enthusiasm Needs Implementation

> A personal note from A. Sai Shivanand for anyone who feels lost at the beginning of college.

In my first year, I was excited about almost everything. I wanted to explore every possible area of computer science: cybersecurity, game development, AI/ML, UI/UX, web development, IoT, and more.

Exploration is not a bad thing. In fact, it helped me discover what exists. But I mostly explored these areas at the surface level. Even after completing first year, I still had not figured out what I truly wanted to pursue.

I also have a few uncomfortable truths to admit:

- My CGPA is not where I want it to be.
- I procrastinate more than I should.
- I often get excited about an idea but struggle with consistent implementation.
- Enthusiasm feels productive, but it is not a substitute for practice and finished work.

## What I wish I had understood earlier

**You do not need to master every domain immediately.** Explore widely, but eventually choose one direction to practise seriously for a meaningful period of time.

A simple approach is:

1. Explore a field briefly.
2. Talk to someone who actually works or learns in that field.
3. Try a small hands-on task.
4. Decide whether you want to continue.
5. Spend consistent time building instead of endlessly switching.

Your direction can change. That is normal. The important part is to turn curiosity into implementation.

## Talk to people—even if you feel awkward

I am a big introvert. Before college, I was especially shy about talking to people I did not know, including girls and teachers. I am still learning how to communicate confidently.

But I made a decision when I entered college: I would try to talk to people instead of staying completely inside my comfort zone.

So here is my advice:

- Talk to classmates, seniors, and people from different branches.
- Ask what they are learning and how they started.
- Ask doubts—even the doubts you think are dumb.
- Talk to teachers about concepts, projects, research, and career paths.
- Do not wait until you feel perfectly confident.

Most people who know something today were once beginners too.

## Be curious, but be accountable

I am also a backchod by nature, and sometimes I have treated academics too casually. I have even tried emotional blackmail with professors for marks. That may sound funny among friends, but it is not a good long-term strategy.

A better approach is to take responsibility:

- Attend and participate honestly.
- Prepare before asking for help.
- Accept feedback without making excuses.
- Improve your work instead of only negotiating for marks.
- Respect teachers and their time.

Humour and fun are part of college life. So are discipline and accountability.

## A message to freshers

If you came to college without much computer experience, do not feel inferior. Start with the basics:

- Learn how files, folders, browsers, and operating systems work.
- Pick one beginner-friendly programming language.
- Learn what Git and GitHub are.
- Try Linux or a terminal gradually.
- Build tiny things and document them.
- Find one or two people who are willing to learn with you.

You do not need a perfect roadmap on day one. You need a first step, followed by another one.

## The lesson I am still learning

**Enthusiasm opens the door. Implementation creates progress. Consistency creates evidence.**

I am still working on procrastination, focus, communication, academics, and actually finishing what I start. Medhira Research is partly my way of learning these things in public and helping others avoid some of my mistakes.

If you are also figuring things out, you are welcome here.
